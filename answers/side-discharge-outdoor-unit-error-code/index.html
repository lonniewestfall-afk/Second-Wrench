Side-Discharge Outdoor Unit Error Code: What It Means

Last updated October 15, 2026.

Most outdoor-unit codes mean a part needs a technician. A few have a real outdoor cause you can check from a few steps back: something blocking the air, snow or ice at the fan, or normal defrost. Write the code down, look from outside, and don’t reset it. That gives your technician a head start.

First, stop for a hazard

Something unsafe is happening now (burning smell, smoke, sparks, gas smell, an alarm, water or ice at electrical parts, or someone at risk): stop checking. We’ll show you where to get help when you start the guided check.

How to read the code

If a code is already showing on the thermostat, remote, wall control, or app, write it down or take a photo. Don’t open the outdoor unit to look for a code, and don’t open service menus or clear codes.

Write the code down exactly as it’s shown, or take a photo of it. For a blinking light, count the blinks for a full minute. A short phone video helps.

Causes you can check, most likely first

  1. Normal defrost (not a fault). Carrier 38MURA shows DF; Bosch Edge shows dfx. “It is normal to display DF or FC, not a malfunction” (Carrier IM-38MURA-02 p. 18). In cold weather, a cloud of steam from the outdoor unit during defrost can be normal. If you’re not sure it’s steam, or you smell burning, treat it as smoke. A normal defrost usually lasts about 5 to 15 minutes, and some models finish sooner. Ice that is still there after about an hour of running is not normal. Call a licensed HVAC professional.
  2. Blocked air. Side-discharge units blow air out the front and pull it in at the back and side, so a fence, shrubs or stored items in front cause the unit to re-breathe hot air. Carrier lists at least 24 in. in front for 38MURA (IM-38MURA-02 p. 8). Bosch Edge lists 12 in. at the inlet and 24 in. above (IOM p. 12). These numbers are only for the models named. Your unit’s installation manual lists its own numbers. Check your unit’s manual. They describe how the unit should be installed. They aren’t a check of your unit.
  3. Ice, snow or debris at the fan. Don’t try to stop, turn, or free the fan, even with the system off. Don’t put hands, sticks, or tools through the grille or into the fins, even with the power off. Parts inside the outdoor unit can stay charged after the power is off, and wind turning the fan can charge them again.
  4. Too cold or too hot outside. The manufacturer describes this code as normal operation or as running in a limited range. If heating or cooling has stopped, or the house is getting uncomfortable, call a licensed HVAC professional now. If not, tell your technician about it.
  5. Everything else (sensors, boards, compressor, voltage, refrigerant). The display on the board inside the outdoor unit is for technicians. Reaching it means removing panels. Refrigerant work needs an EPA-certified technician. If you hear hissing, turn the system Off at the thermostat. Don’t touch the pipes, valves, or caps.

What not to do

Don’t clear the code, delete the error history, reset the unit, or switch the power off and on to make the code go away. The code and its history help your technician.

This guide doesn’t cover resetting breakers or turning the disconnect back on. You can look, but don’t switch anything. If a breaker looks tripped or the disconnect is off, leave it as it is and call a licensed HVAC professional or electrician. It may have been turned off on purpose, or because of a problem a pro should find. A breaker that trips again is a warning sign. Don’t reset it.

It’s not normal if

  • Not normal: a solid block of ice that keeps growing, or a grille iced shut.
  • Hissing at the pipe connections (not the whoosh during a defrost), or fresh oily spots at the pipes or valves.
  • The unit is tipping, falling, or pulling away from the wall.

If the house is cold

Before we look at the outdoor unit: is the house getting too cold for anyone in it? If anyone at home is a baby, an older adult, or has a medical need, or the indoor temperature keeps dropping, getting them warm comes first.

Never heat with an oven, stove, grill, or generator indoors (carbon monoxide). If you use an electric space heater, plug it straight into a wall outlet, follow its maker’s instructions, and keep it clear of anything that can burn.

Is this normal?

Some outdoor units wait several minutes before starting. For example, Bosch lists a 7-minute startup delay on IDS Edge multi-zone systems (IOM p. 53). Switching the power off and on can restart the wait.

These code meanings come from manufacturers’ documents for some models. Your model may differ. A code explains what the unit detected. It isn’t a diagnosis.
