Furnace Turns On, Then Shuts Off

Last updated October 29, 2026.

If you smell gas or rotten eggs in the air, not just from the hot water, leave the house now and call the gas utility or 911 from outside. Don’t touch light switches or phones, or use lighters, matches, or anything that could spark or flame while you’re inside.

If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, or anyone has a headache, dizziness, nausea, or drowsiness, get everyone outside and call 911 from outside. Don’t go back in to turn equipment off.

Short cycling means the furnace starts, then stops quickly, or it keeps trying. Don’t stand at the furnace and restart it over and over. Each retry can be another attempt to light.

Causes, most common first

  1. Thermostat settings. Fan On, a setpoint only a hair above the room, or a schedule that keeps changing the call for heat.
  2. A dirty filter or a blocked return. The furnace can overheat and open a limit. You don’t reset or bypass that limit. You can change a filter you reach from the floor and clear a return grille.
  3. Snow, ice, or a blocked vent pipe, or a condensate tube that is kinked or frozen. A pressure switch can shut the furnace off when it can’t breathe or can’t drain.
  4. A flame sensor, a failing igniter, or a limit that keeps opening. Those parts are inside the furnace. That’s a pro job after the checks above.

Checks you can do

  1. Look at the thermostat you already use. Don’t take it off the wall. Set mode to Heat, fan to Auto, and the setpoint a few degrees above the room temperature. Fan On blows room-temperature air between heating cycles, which can feel cold. Some thermostats wait a few minutes before they start. Then wait one full cycle. Don’t change installer settings such as cycles per hour to force it to stay on.
  2. Turn the system Off at the thermostat and wait until the blower stops. That does not cut power. Use only a tool-free filter slot or return grille you can reach from the floor. Read the size printed on the filter frame, put in one filter of that size, and point the airflow arrow toward the furnace. Close the slot. Don’t open a burner door to find a filter.
  3. Walk the returns and supplies. Furniture, rugs, and closed doors can choke a return. Open the supplies in the rooms you use. Don’t close a pile of vents to push more air to one room. That can make the short cycling worse.
  4. On a high-efficiency furnace, two plastic pipes on an outside wall bring air in and send exhaust out. From the ground, brush snow and leaves away from both ends by hand or with a soft broom, and clear a wide area around them. Leave ice that is stuck to a pipe. Stop if a pipe is cracked, loose, pulled apart, or has ice inside it.
  5. A high-efficiency furnace makes water. A clear or white tube outside the cabinet carries it to a floor drain or a small pump. Look from outside the furnace. If a tube you can reach without tools is kinked, gently straighten it by hand. Don’t pull it off. Stop if the tube is frozen, cracked, or you would have to open the furnace.
  6. If a light is blinking, don’t cut power. Count it for a full minute and use the blinking light page.

Stop and call

Stop using the furnace if you hear a bang or boom when it starts, you see soot or scorch marks, or the flame is somewhere it shouldn’t be. Don’t open a door to watch the burner. Don’t hold a door switch in with tape, and don’t jumper a safety. Furnace breakers are never reset. Look at the breaker marked for the furnace. If the handle is in the middle or off, leave it and call a licensed HVAC professional. Never open the breaker panel cover or a disconnect box.

If the blower never stops and the air is cold, that’s a different problem: blowing cold air. If nothing starts at all, use furnace not turning on.
