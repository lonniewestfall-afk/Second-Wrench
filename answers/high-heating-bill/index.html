Why Is My Heating Bill So High?

Last updated October 29, 2026.

A high bill is not, by itself, a failed furnace. Start with settings and airflow you can see. This page does not say what your bill should be, and it does not compare you to a national average.

Checks that change the bill

  1. Setpoint and schedule. The U.S. Department of Energy Energy Saver guide says you can save as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling by turning the thermostat down 7°–10°F for 8 hours a day in fall and winter, and up in spring and summer. The same guide says not to do that setback on a heat pump unless the thermostat is made for heat pumps. A deep setback can call on backup electric heat and wipe out the savings.
  2. Fan On. Auto runs the blower with a call for heat. On runs it all the time and moves room-temperature air between cycles. That feels cool and uses more electricity. Set Auto unless a pro told you otherwise.
  3. Emergency Heat or Aux on a heat pump. Those strips heat with electricity and usually cost more to run. Leave them off for everyday heat. Use them only the way the guided check describes while you wait for a pro, then turn them off.
  4. A dirty filter. ENERGY STAR says a dirty filter makes the system work harder. Check monthly in winter and change it at least every 3 months, or when it looks dirty. Steps are on the filter size page.
  5. Blocked returns and closed supplies. Furniture against a return, or many closed vents, makes the blower work harder. Open the vents in the rooms you use. Don’t close most of them to force heat upstairs.
  6. Snow on a heat pump or on furnace vent pipes. A buried outdoor unit or a blocked exhaust pipe can stop heating or push the system onto backup heat. Brush loose snow away from the ground around the unit, or from both furnace pipes, from the ground. Don’t chip ice. Never use a pressure washer on your outdoor unit. It can flatten the fins and damage the coil.
  7. Drafts you can already see. A gap under a door or a window that doesn’t latch lets heat out. A door sweep you already own is a house item, not a furnace repair. Duct sealing in an attic is a pro or a careful manual job, not a step on this page.

Read your own bill

Compare this month with the same month you have a bill for, and look at the units used, not only the dollar total. A rate change and a cold snap can both raise the total. If usage jumped and the checks above didn’t change anything, call a licensed HVAC professional. Bring the thermostat settings and whether Emergency Heat has been on. Don’t open the furnace to hunt for a cause.

If the bill is hard to pay

Ask your utility what payment plans it offers. The Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program is described at LIHEAP. Who qualifies, and for how much, is set where you live. This page does not say you qualify. A quote to replace the system is a separate decision: how to get a second opinion.

Start the guided check and choose Furnace or air handler, or Heat pump if the outdoor unit heats the house. For thermostat schedules, choose Something else, then Thermostat or settings problem.
